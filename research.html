Research Projects

Marine chemistry to sensor development, planetary science to acid rain

My scientific research career has contained multitudes, and I have undertaken projects from a wide range of fields including environmental chemistry, geochemistry, marine chemistry, planetary science, and sensor development. My current work focuses on understanding controls on the movement and fate of methane (CH4) and carbon dioxide (CO2) in difficult-to-measure aquatic ecosystems in both modern settings and the geological past.

Current Projects

Methane cold seeps, past and present: Anoxic marine sediments host the largest pool of methane on Earth's surface. While much of this methane is consumed anaerobically by microbes as it diffuses upwards through sediments, cold seeps are locations where focused fluid flow or high methane concentrations are able to overcome the microbial filter and reach the sediment-seawater interface. These cold seeps are oases in the deep sea, and their relatively abundant methane supplies energy to unique chemosynthetic environments full of endemic species. The anaerobic oxidation of methane (AOM) at these seeps increases alkalinity and drives the precipitation of methane-derived authigenic carbonates (MDACs) that serve as hardgrounds for sessile species such as tubeworms and deep sea corals.

My research on methane cold seeps takes two forms: reconstructing paleo-AOM rates from seep carbonates and mapping/quantifying methane fluxes and dissolved methane concentrations. During my doctoral research, I developed a numerical model that showed that the clumped isotope composition – specifically ∆47 – of these carbonates can be used to constrain the AOM rates during their precipitation and published this novel proxy in PNAS. I am currently applying this relationship to clumped isotope measurements I made on seep carbonates from the Norwegian Margin and South China Sea to explore how AOM rates (and potentially CH4 flux) may respond to changes in climate on geological timescales.

The other focus of my cold seep research is aimed at discovering new seeps and quantifying CH4 fluxes and dissolved CH4 storage at seep sites. I have participated in two oceanographic expeditions aimed at discovering new seeps, and my role has been to deploy cutting-edge sensor technology to map dissolved methane concentrations at these sites. These measurements have not only allowed directly verification of methane seepage at both newly-discovered and known seep sites, but they have also provided the ability to quantify the near-seafloor storage of CH4 at seep environments. Combining these measurements with ship- and ROV-based imaging has also allowed me to make margin-scale estimates of CH4 flux and storage, such as along the Chilean Margin which was recently published in Geophysical Research Letters.

Greenhouse gases in shallow aquatic environments: CH4 and CO2 cycling in shallow aquatic environments are both critical parts of the global carbon cycle, but it is difficult to constrain relevant processes and water-air flux in these environments due to high temporal and spatial variability. Such environments can be both sources and sinks of carbon on both local and regional scales, making high resolution in situ measurements in time and space necessary to properly quantify production and consumption as well as atmospheric flux. To improve such constraints, I deploy cutting edge measurement technologies in the field across a wide range of scales and environments, from deep submarine canyons to estuaries and lakes.

The three focal sites for this work have been the Biobío Canyon offshore Chile, the Hudson River Estuary in New York, and the Cambridge Bay region of Victoria Island, Nunavut. I measured dissolved CH4 in both the shallow and deep portions of the Biobío Canyon during three dives as part of an oceanographic expedition along the central Chilean Margin. These measurements identified various sources and manifestations of CH4 in the canyon environment, including production of CH4 in the oxygen-deficient zone following algal bloom die-off, midwater CH4 plumes possibly linked to distal seepage, and microseepage on the canyon walls hosting seep-associated fauna.

My work in the Hudson River Estuary and Cambridge Bay focuses on developing high resolution maps of CH4 and CO2 along the land to sea continuum in both space and time. The goal of this research is to understand the physical processes dominating the distribution and transportation of both gases and leverage these results to improve atmospheric flux estimates for these environments. Preliminary work on the Hudson River Estuary has identified the role of spring and neap tidal cycles in regulating CH4 distribution and CH4 and CO2 fluxes.

Sensor development and deployment: Measuring dissolved gas concentrations is difficult, and the difficulty increases significantly the deeper in the ocean you go. As a member of the Chemical Sensors Laboratory at the Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution, I develop and operate novel dissolved gas sensors in a variety of aquatic environments. My current development work entails altering the sensor for aqueous gases in the environment (SAGE) to be capable of measuring the concentration and δ13C of dissolved inorganic carbon. Our goal is to produce a fiber optic based field-deployable instrument capable of measuring DIC and its carbon isotopic composition to better than 1‰ precision at depths of up to 6,000 m.

In addition to cutting-edge field sensors, I have developed prototype benchtop systems for measuring the clumped isotope composition of CO2 using optical techniques with the aim of improving measurement speed, decreasing sample size requirements, and moving towards cheaper and more accessible instruments. One such prototype, published in RCMS, was a proof of concept that both carbon-oxygen and oxygen-oxygen clumping could be measured simultaneously without requiring either a second laser or cryogenic temperatures. The second prototype built off of the Chemical Sensor Laboratory's widespread use of hollow core fiber instruments to measure clumped isotope ratios on nanomoles of CO2 as highlighted in Optics Express.

Past Projects

Aerosol-induced fractionation on Titan: As part of my work at NASA's Goddard Space Flight Center, I conducted gas recirculation experiments studying the formation of aerosols in a Titan-like atmosphere exposed to far-UV light. During this experiment, which was spearheaded by Dr. Melissa Ugelow, we continually monitored higher order hydrocarbon concentrations and δ13C ratios in methane, ethane, propane in both hydrogen-rich and hydrogen-poor environments. These experiments helped clarify the photochemical pathways for aerosol formation on Titan and what influence hydrogen plays in regulating Titan's atmospheric composition. Results of this work were published in ACS Earth and Space Chemistry.

Preservation of organic signatures on Mars: As a member of the Curiosity Rover science team, I explored the capability for the Martian surface to preserve possible organic carbon and nitrogen signatures from hypothetical ancient microbial life. I was part of the Sample Analysis at Mars (SAM) instrument suite team, and in that role I measured the chemical and isotopic composition of Martian surface samples and designed and synthesized isotopic standards for the SAM testbed. In addition to conducting work on Mars, I measured carbon and oxygen isotope ratios of stromatolites to characterize organic carbon in support of a project led by Ashley Murphy (then a Ph.D. student at Rutgers University) published in Sedimentary Geology. I also measured carbon and nitrogen isotope ratios in synthetic Martian analog smectite clays for research led by Dr. Slavka Andrejkovičová focused on identifying possible signatures of reduced nitrogen during the pyrolysis of Martian surface clays that was published in JGR Planets.

Acid rain buffering in New England: In my first foray into geochemistry, I spent a year conducting research in Hopkins Memorial Forest in Williamstown, MA. As part of this work, I worked up 30 years of chemical measurements from rainfall and two streams which showed both a long-term increase in rainfall and stream pH and significant differences between throughfall and bulk rainfall chemistry. The stream-focused work has been published in Hydrological Processes, and I hope to one day publish a focused manuscript on the rainfall results.
